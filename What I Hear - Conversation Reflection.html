What I Hear

A structured reflection for understanding the message beneath the message

BAGC6 Executive Reset  ·  Beyond Limits
This is not about deciding who was right. It is a private space to slow a conversation down and look at it honestly. Most conflict does not come from what was said. It comes from the meaning we attached to it, and the emotion that meaning produced. Work through each step in order. When you can see the whole exchange clearly, you can choose a different response. One rule for this exercise: separate what actually happened from what you made it mean. The words someone said are a fact. What those words meant about you, about them, or about the relationship is an interpretation, and interpretations can be examined.
1
Set the Scene

Start with the facts only. Name the conversation and what was actually said or done, the part a camera would have recorded. No interpretation yet.

1Who was the conversation with, and what was it about?
2What were the actual words or actions? Quote them as closely as you can.
Example: “You do not take responsibility.” She said it once, near the end of the call.
2
What I Heard

Now the interpretation. Between their words and your reaction, your mind translated the message into something about you. Name that translation plainly, because it is usually where the real hurt lives.

1What did I hear those words to mean? What did I tell myself they were really saying?
2What accusation or judgement did I feel underneath it?
Example: I heard “you are selfish and you always let people down.” That is not what she said. It is what I heard.
3
What I Felt

Emotions are information, not instructions. Name what rose up, where you felt it, and the belief sitting underneath it. The belief is what turns a comment into a wound.

1What did I feel, and where did I feel it in my body?
2What was I assuming their words meant about how they see me, or feel about me?
Trace the chain

Notice how it links: a fact, then a meaning, then a feeling, then a conclusion. It sounds like “they said X, so they must think Y, so they do not care, so I am not loved.” Each arrow is a choice, not a certainty. You will come back and test these.

4
Step Into Their Shoes

This is the heart of the exercise. Put your own version to one side and genuinely consider theirs. Nobody experiences themselves as the villain. What did the same moment look like from where they were standing?

Fill in both sides. Seeing them next to each other is what shifts the picture.
What I felt
What they may have felt
1What pressure, fear, or difficult position might they have been in that I did not see?
2What might they have actually meant that I have not yet considered?
Example: She was caught between two people she loves and had no way of winning with either of us. “You do not take responsibility” may have been her asking me to stop putting her in the middle.
5
Read It Differently

Hold both readings side by side: the one that hurt, and one that is just as possible. You are not forcing yourself to be positive. You are proving to yourself that your first reading was a choice, not a fact.

The story I first told
A kinder, equally-true reading
1What did I add to their words that they never actually said?
2If a friend I trust had heard this same exchange, how might they have understood it?
6
Respond Differently

Now turn reflection into words you could actually use. Speak from your own experience, assume good intent, and describe what you heard without accusing. A useful habit is to say the repair, then stop, full stop, and let them respond.

1What do I now want to understand or check with them, rather than assume?
2How could I say what I heard and felt, without blame? (Try: “When … happened, I found myself thinking … and I am sure this is not what you meant.”)
3What is the new thought I want to carry forward about this person or this conversation?
Two words to avoid

Try not to use “but” or “however” when you repair this. Both quietly erase everything that came before them. Use “and”, or simply start a new sentence.

You do not hear people. You hear the meaning you gave them.

Between their words and your reaction, there is always a space. In that space, you get to choose what it meant.

This reflection is how you learn to use it.

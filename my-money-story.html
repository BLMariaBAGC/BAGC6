My Money Story

Everything you believe about money was learned. Not one of those beliefs was original. They were absorbed from the people and environments around you, long before you had the capacity to question them.

Your relationship with money today, how you earn it, how you spend it, how you feel about it, is not a reflection of your intelligence or your worth. It is a map of your earliest programming.

This exercise surfaces where that programming came from. That is the first step to changing it.

You did not choose your money story. But you can choose what happens next.

There are no right or wrong answers here. Write what is actually true, not what sounds healthy or evolved. The more honest you are, the more useful this exercise becomes.

The Early Environment

Reflect on the home you grew up in. Answer as honestly as you can. These reflections are private and for you alone.

Growing up, was money talked about with abundance or scarcity?
What was the emotional atmosphere around money in your home?
Were your caregivers over-spenders, balanced, or too tight with money?
Was money a source of arguments or tension in your home?
Did you ever feel different from other children because of money, either having less or more?
Was generosity modelled around you, or was giving seen as risky or foolish?
The Messages You Received

Select everything you heard, directly or indirectly, growing up. These may have been said out loud, implied through behaviour, or simply the unspoken atmosphere of your home.

Your Pattern

Take your time with these questions. They are asking you to name patterns you may never have put into words before.

What was the single dominant money belief you absorbed growing up?
Whose voice do you hear most when you think about money?
How old were you when you first became aware that money was something to worry about?
What financial behaviour do you repeat today that you learned from watching others?
The Subconscious Conflict
Why the Subconscious Keeps Money Away

If the programming you absorbed says that rich people are corrupt, greedy, or evil, then becoming wealthy would, in your subconscious logic, make you that person. So the mind, wanting to protect your sense of being good, keeps money away. The drive to stay good and the drive to stay broke can feel like exactly the same thing.

Do you notice yourself self-sabotaging when things start to go well financially?
Do you feel uncomfortable receiving money, charging what you are worth, or asking for more?
Is there a part of you that associates financial struggle with being a good, honest, humble person?

Money is neither good nor bad. It is a tool. It is used by people, some bad, some good. The money itself carries no moral value. The person using it does.

Challenging the Assumption

These questions invite you to test the beliefs you have been carrying, using evidence from your own experience and perspective.

Name three things money has funded that you believe were genuinely good
Name one wealthy or financially successful person you genuinely respect
What would a good person do with significant financial resources?
What good would you do with more money?

Your parents and caregivers had their own programming. They were not wrong. They were passing on what they received. Their beliefs about money were formed in a different world, a different time, and a different set of circumstances.

You get to choose what you carry forward.
